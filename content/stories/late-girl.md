Her eyes were beautiful. She had the most gorgeous long blond hair, shiny, clean, tidy, even length with no straggly bits at the ends. She swayed gently with the rocking of the tube train as it thundered underneath busy London streets. He kept looking at her pretty face, then away in case she thought he was staring. He wondered how far she was travelling, where she lived, whether she was single, what kind of music she liked, the sort of movies she enjoyed. He tried to imagine spending time with her. Oh, how those skin tight jeans tormented him. He hankered. He longed. He imagined. Then he worried that she was becoming aware of his leering glances. He thought that she probably disliked being objectified but, despite his guilt, he knew he was going to continue stealing glances just because she was so gorgeous.

She was indeed aware of his constant, twitchy glances. She made up her mind to get out at the next stop, if he didn't. She hated creeps who stared at her like this in public places. It irritated her that people like him, men like him, felt no compunction about "eyeing up the girls". As the train pulled into Regent's Park, where only lost tourists alight, she waited a moment to be sure he was remaining seated, then jumped off just before the doors closed to wait for the next train. She wondered whether he would be waiting at the next station for her...

The train accelerated away along the grubby tunnel leaving her in the suddenly quiet station and she realised that deserted stations were not a good place for a single girl to hang around in. She wished she had not got off now. How long would she have to wait for another train? She was not sure about buses in London, having always taken the tube or a taxi if she was out for the evening. The station Tannoy crackled and buzzed into life and a male voice with heavy foreign accent mumbled some incomprehensible words for no apparent reason, but it didn't matter as the increasing rush of wind and roar of the next train rushed in to rescue her from this silent part of London. She vowed not to be so stupid next time, and to ignore the mousey cowards that stole furtive glances on trains. Next time, she'll just move out of the line of sight instead of making herself late.

The Station Master watched her through his TV monitors, wondering why she had stepped off one train only to wait for the next one. As he idly scanned the various monochrome monitors that enabled him to master the station without straying on to the platform or ever actually having to meet a passenger, he noticed that something was between the lines that was not there a few minutes ago. He could not make it out on the screen so he waited until the next train had whisked the lonely girl on her way, then came out of his concealed room like a guilty mouse in a food cupboard. Peering over the edge of the platform, he saw a shiny stainless steel saucepan lying on the oily concrete, upside down. He had no idea how it had got there, but it appeared to be doing no harm to anything, so he returned quickly to his quiet room from where he could make his garbled announcements. He set himself the task of working out what accent to fake for the next pointless announcement.

The saucepan had, in fact, been there for two weeks. It was remarkable that the unobservant station master had noticed it at all. In the days that it had lain there, several rats had used it as cover for their forays into the junk that passengers discarded, but otherwise it had lain there, undisturbed, quietly and slowly corroding. A train thundered over it and a handful of passengers got off. One hung around on the platform, having got off in search of a girl he had seen getting off from here some minutes before. He was still thinking about her. She had jumped from out of his line of sight just as the doors had closed and he had got off at the following station and returned here to look for her. A fool’s errand, he knew. Obviously, she was not going to hang around the dusty platform, and there was any number of routes she could have taken on leaving the station. He felt sheepish and stupid, so after waiting ten minutes, staring at the saucepan, he went to the other platform to continue his journey, much later for this idiotic diversion, and having chastised himself for letting his libido lead him on a wild goose chase. He felt that he ought to know better and promised himself that next time he travelled on public transport he would bring a book to keep his mind off his fellow passengers.

He had no work today and was going to the gallery to see if he could find something there that might help him to understand the direction that modern art had seemed, to him, to have lost.
